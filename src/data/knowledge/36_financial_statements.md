# Financial Statements: Reading the Numbers

## The Three Key Statements

Every company (and you!) can be analyzed with three statements:

1. **Income Statement**: Profitability
2. **Balance Sheet**: Assets vs Liabilities
3. **Cash Flow Statement**: Cash movement

## Income Statement (P&L)

Shows profit/loss over period:

### Example: Apple 2023
- **Revenue**: $394 billion (what customers paid)
- **Cost of sales**: $220 billion (cost to make products)
- **Gross profit**: $174 billion (revenue - COGS)
- **Operating expenses**: $60 billion (R&D, marketing, admin)
- **Operating income**: $114 billion
- **Other income**: $3 billion (interest, investments)
- **Taxes**: $16 billion
- **Net income**: $97 billion (bottom line profit)

### Key Metrics

**Gross Margin** = Gross profit ÷ Revenue
- Apple: $174B ÷ $394B = 44%
- Healthy for tech/manufacturing

**Operating Margin** = Operating income ÷ Revenue
- Apple: $114B ÷ $394B = 29%
- Shows efficiency of operations

**Net Margin** = Net income ÷ Revenue
- Apple: $97B ÷ $394B = 25%
- Shows true profitability

## Balance Sheet (Snapshot)

Shows financial position at a date:

### Assets (What You Own)
**Current** (Can convert to cash within 1 year):
- Cash: $29 billion
- Accounts receivable: $60 billion (owed to company)
- Inventory: $24 billion (products to sell)
- Total current: $113 billion

**Non-Current** (Long-term):
- Equipment: $100 billion
- Buildings: $40 billion
- Goodwill: $50 billion (from acquisitions)
- Total non-current: $190 billion

**Total Assets: $303 billion**

### Liabilities (What You Owe)
**Current** (Due within 1 year):
- Accounts payable: $50 billion
- Current debt: $30 billion
- Total current: $80 billion

**Non-Current** (Long-term debt):
- Long-term debt: $90 billion

**Total Liabilities: $170 billion**

### Equity (Net Worth)
**Equity = Assets - Liabilities**
- Apple equity: $303B - $170B = $133 billion
- Means Apple owns $133B (shareholders own this)

## Cash Flow Statement

Tracks actual cash movement (not profit):

### Three Sections

**Operating Cash Flow** (Cash from business):
- Net income: $97 billion
- Depreciation (non-cash): $12 billion
- Changes in working capital: -$5 billion
- **Operating cash flow: $104 billion**

**Investing Cash Flow** (Cash for investments):
- Buying equipment: -$10 billion
- Buying other companies: -$20 billion
- **Investing cash flow: -$30 billion**

**Financing Cash Flow** (Cash from loans/stock):
- Debt repayment: -$15 billion
- Stock buyback: -$70 billion
- Dividends: -$15 billion
- **Financing cash flow: -$100 billion**

**Net Cash Change**: $104B - $30B - $100B = -$26B

(Cash decreased $26B, but still has $29B cash)

## Why These Matter

### Income Statement
- **For investors**: Shows profitability
- **For analysts**: Trend analysis (growing?)
- **For business**: Efficiency

### Balance Sheet
- **For lenders**: Can you repay debt?
- **For investors**: Net worth
- **For business**: Have capital for growth?

### Cash Flow
- **For creditors**: Can you pay bills?
- **Most important**: Company can be profitable but cash-broke!

## Key Ratios

### Profitability
**Return on Assets (ROA)** = Net income ÷ Total assets
- Apple: $97B ÷ $303B = 32%
- Good if >10%

**Return on Equity (ROE)** = Net income ÷ Equity
- Apple: $97B ÷ $133B = 73%
- Good if >15%

### Liquidity (Can Pay Bills?)
**Current Ratio** = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
- Apple: $113B ÷ $80B = 1.4
- Good if >1.0 (means can cover current liabilities)

**Quick Ratio** = (Current assets - Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities
- More conservative
- Apple: ($113B - $24B) ÷ $80B = 1.1
- Good if >1.0

### Leverage (Debt Level)
**Debt-to-Equity** = Total debt ÷ Equity
- Apple: $120B ÷ $133B = 0.9
- Low debt is safer (Apple is safe)

**Debt-to-Assets** = Total debt ÷ Total assets
- Apple: $120B ÷ $303B = 40%
- Reasonable (not overleveraged)

## Financial Statement Red Flags

❌ **Revenue declining**: Business losing customers  
❌ **Expenses growing faster than revenue**: Margin shrinking  
❌ **Inventory rising**: Products not selling  
❌ **Debt exploding**: Danger zone  
❌ **Operating cash flow negative**: Can't fund operations  
❌ **Current ratio <1.0**: Can't pay short-term bills  
❌ **Margins declining**: Losing competitive edge  

## Reading Financial Statements

### Step 1: Income Statement
- Is company profitable? (Net income positive?)
- Are margins stable/improving?
- Are expenses controlled?

### Step 2: Balance Sheet
- Do assets exceed liabilities? (Positive equity)
- Is debt reasonable? (Debt-to-equity <1.5)
- Is cash adequate? (Months of operations)

### Step 3: Cash Flow
- Is operating cash flow positive? (Most important)
- Can they fund growth? (CapEx from cash flow)
- Are dividends sustainable? (From cash, not debt)

### Step 4: Trends
- Growing revenue? (Year over year)
- Improving margins? (Operational efficiency)
- Increasing cash? (Real growth)

## Company Valuation Using Statements

### P/E Ratio (Price to Earnings)
Stock price ÷ Earnings per share

- Apple stock: $190
- Earnings per share: $6.05
- P/E: 31x
- Means investors pay $31 for every $1 of annual profit

**Interpretation**:
- High P/E (>20): Growth expected or expensive
- Low P/E (<15): Value or distressed

### Price-to-Book
Stock price ÷ Book value per share

Apple:
- Stock: $190
- Book value per share: $27
- P/B: 7x
- Investors pay $7 for every $1 of net worth

### Free Cash Flow Yield
Operating cash flow ÷ Market cap

- Apple operating cash: $104B
- Market cap: $3 trillion
- Yield: 3.5%
- Is it cheap or expensive?

## Key Takeaway
Income statement shows profitability. Balance sheet shows net worth. Cash flow shows true financial health. Many profitable companies fail due to cash flow (most important). Ratios show efficiency and safety. Red flags indicate deterioration. Apply same analysis to stocks you buy or your own finances. Warren Buffett reads financials carefully. Boring but crucial for investing success.
